# What has to be explained

At hidden phase 10 the Hi KO mice earn 5.5 rewards against 15.2 in WT (p = 0.004). At cued
phase 10 — the same mice, the same days, the same arena, with a marker on the goal — they earn
29.7 against 30.7 (p = 1.000). The loss is locked to the rotation rather than the calendar: every
measure that declines across the four hidden probes is flat across the four cued ones.

So the question is not whether these mice are impaired. One number and its control settle that.
The question is what the missing rewards are made of, and the task was built to answer it: it
records where the mouse went, how fast it was moving when it got there, and how long it stayed.

Everything below comes from the full report, *Pten KO mosaicism and spatial accuracy: a
behavioural interrogation* (version 3), which holds the methods, the controls and every statistic
quoted here. Figure numbers in brackets are its own.

# 1. Two phenotypes, not one dose

Lo mice are the slowest of all three groups to learn that the marker predicts reward — 21.2
sessions against 8.8, with no overlap between the groups — and they keep the remembered place when
a marker is available (+12.0 points, p = 0.016). Hi mice take the rule up at the WT rate, median
11.5 sessions, inside the WT range of 7 to 13, and show no place preference at all (−4.2,
p = 0.58).

The two KO groups are displaced from WT in close to orthogonal directions, and the slowest
learners have the fewest KO cells. The mosaic fraction is not a dial on severity. It is closer to
a switch between strategies.

Note what this does **not** say. Lo mice are not unable to learn the cue rule — all five reach
criterion — they take about two and a half times as long. It is a rate difference, and that is
what makes the dissociation work: the group that is slowest on the cued task is the group whose
place memory is intact.

# 2. The break is at the stop, not the approach

Everything in this section is one deficit measured at two scales: the mouse does not commit to a
place. At the goal that is paying now, it arrives and leaves. Across the places that used to pay,
it visits and moves on.

## 2a. At the goal that is paying now

![](figsv3/fig16a_arrival_runs.svg){width=6.3in}

*Figure 3. Arriving at the goal: what the runs look like. **a** Every directed run into the hidden goal at phase 10 for one mouse per group, in goal-centred coordinates, rotated so the approach comes from the left and coloured by speed; the dot marks where the mouse was 1.2 s after entering — inside the zone (green) is a reward, outside (red) is a miss. **b** One run per mouse, the run whose slowing ratio is closest to that mouse's median; a dot every 0.1 s, so the dots bunch where the mouse is slow. All three panels share one axis range, so the goal circles are the same size and the runs are drawn to the same scale. **c** Mean speed around every entry into the current goal — directed runs and chance crossings alike — at the trained location, the last rotation and the cued last rotation; mean ± SE across mice; the shaded band is the 1.2 s the mouse must stay in the zone to earn the reward. The p value compares the mean speed inside that window, Hi vs WT (Mann-Whitney).* [report Figure 8]

Getting there is intact. Launch distance and heading to the goal are unchanged, and the shape
of the approach is normal: with every directed run scaled to its own approach maximum, the speed
profile against distance from the goal does not differ between groups under a permutation test
across the whole profile with max-statistic correction (p = 0.53), nor within animals between cued
and hidden (p = 0.83). Hi mice decelerate on approach like everyone else.

What fails is the arrival. Averaging speed around every entry into the goal — chance crossings as
well as runs the mouse aimed at — the Hi mice are moving at **16.4 cm/s through the 1.2 s window
that earns a pellet, against 11.5 in WT (p = 0.0005)**. At the trained location the same comparison
gives p = 0.18, and with the marker restored, p = 0.18. They reach the place and pass through it.

The dwell measures say the same thing in other units — 59 s of pausing per session against 134,
0.14 rewards per entrance against 0.32 — and they are not independent of it. The reward rule *is*
"stop in the zone for 1.2 s", so a mouse that runs through the goal fails every one of them by
construction. They are one measurement seen from several sides, and they are reported here as a
decomposition of the reward loss rather than as a set of separate findings.

Whishaw, Cassel and Jarrard (1995, *J Neurosci* 15:5779) put the same test to fimbria-fornix rats
and their rats passed it: they decelerated at the same distance from the vanished platform as
controls, which is how those authors concluded the rats knew where it was and the deficit lay
elsewhere. Ours fail it, and only at the last moment.

## 2b. Across the places that used to pay

![](figsv3/fig17_former_goals.svg){width=5.6in}

*Figure 4. The set to rule out. Pause time at former goals per session, by phase, with the goal hidden (left) and cued (right). The number of former goals in play grows 0, 1, 2, 3 across the four phases. Faint points are mice, lines are group means ± SE; beneath each panel are the mean per-mouse slopes and the Mann-Whitney comparing those slopes between Hi and WT; numbers above a phase are Hi vs WT at that phase. Pauses at the wall are excluded by the pause definition throughout.* [report Figure 10]

By the last rotation a mouse has four candidate places in its history, and WT mice work
through them: 0, 33, 42 and 82 s of pausing at former goals as the set grows from none to three.
Lo mice do the same (0, 31, 55, 88). Hi mice do not (0, 15, 26, 28). The per-mouse slopes are
+25.3, +28.8 and +9.6 s per phase, and the group × phase comparison is p = 0.003. With the goal
cued the same mice behave alike (p = 0.198).

This is the only result in the report whose shape rather than level is the finding, and the only
one where the informative behaviour is the WT mice's: they search more as the history lengthens,
and the Hi mice do not add to the list.

But the failure is not a failure to look. Braking at former goals is identical in all three groups
(15.2, 15.8 and 14.7 events per 10 m, p = 0.93). Hi mice pass through the old places and slow at
them as often as anyone. What they do not do is stay. It is the same asymmetry as 2a, one scale up:
the approach to a candidate is intact and the commitment to it is not.

*Figure 5. Where they look, with the cue on and off. **a–f** One session per mouse per condition at phase 10, cue present (left) and cue removed (right), for the three mice closest to their group medians across five of the measures in this report (M9 WT, M8 Lo, M5 Hi). **The control that holds both halves together.** Removing the cue makes WT mice pause *more*,
because the goal now has to be remembered (+30 s, p = 0.024), and makes Hi mice pause less (−34 s);
the change differs between the groups at p = 0.043. Nothing about locomotion, motivation or vision
changes between those two sessions. What changes is whether the location has to be held in mind.

# 3. They do not do less, they do more

![](figsv/sm__fig_speed_mode.svg){width=6.5in}

*Figure 6. a: mean speed per probe session by phase, cued (dashed) and hidden (solid), mean ± SE. b: the same comparison within each mouse — speed in the hidden session minus speed in the cued session at the same phase. Negative means the mouse slows down when the marker is removed. Faint points are mice. c: goal-zone entries against rewards at hidden phase 10, one point per mouse, with reference lines at one reward per three and per six entries.* [report Figure 13]

When the cue is removed WT mice slow down — the task has become harder — and Hi mice speed up:
−1.80 cm/s in WT and −1.13 in Lo against +1.69 in Hi (p = 0.001 against WT). They enter the goal
zone as often as WT mice (43.3 against 48.8, p = 0.77) while covering about 29% more ground to do
it.

This is why a found-it-or-didn't measure misleads here rather than merely under-reporting. It
describes a mouse doing less of the right thing. What the mouse is actually doing is a different
thing, more energetically.

# 4. The circuit

![](figsv3/fig15_ied.svg){width=6.2in}

*Figure 7. Interictal discharges. **A** Grand-average IED voltage at baseline and after CNO, on the same colour scale (5 mice; 41 and 131 IEDs); bars above each raster mark the initiation (IP, −1 to 0 ms) and propagation (PP, 2 to 15 ms) windows; beneath, voltage by layer in each window on the same axis, initiation filled and propagation open. **B** Theta excess and IED initiation by layer: baseline theta CSD of IED+ mice minus WT (IED+ 5, WT 10); the CSD of the averaged IED in the initiation window (blue sink, red source); and both profiles scaled to their own maximum, with shading marking GCL2 and MML2.* [report Figure 23]

Hi KO mice start with about twice the WT theta-band current density in the lower blade, at
GCL2 and MML2 where the KO cells and their perforant-path synapses sit (4-layer mean p = 0.043).
CNO silences hM4D-expressing Pten KO granule neurons in slice — spikes per sweep fall by 75%,
two of five neurons stop firing at any current tested (95% CI on the effect −6.9 to −1.6 spikes
per sweep, though at n = 5 the paired test is p = 0.051) — and in vivo it removes most of the theta excess in 6 of 7 Hi mice while leaving WT unchanged. The interictal
discharges arise from the same two layers: the IED's initiation current is a sink in GCL2 with a
source in MML2, and baseline theta CSD in those layers identifies the IED+ mice almost perfectly
(AUC 0.97), an ability it loses after CNO. The continuous current and the intermittent discharge
are two outputs of one lower-blade circuit, and because CNO does not reduce the discharges while
it does normalise the current and the behaviour, the theta-band state is the better candidate for
the route from Pten KO to the memory deficit.

# 5. What to be careful about

- **Six Hi mice and five Lo mice.** Every group statement rests on single-digit n. The
  within-animal cued control is the strongest design in the set precisely because it does not
  depend on between-group variance.
- **Section 2a is one measurement, not six.** Speed through the reward window, pause time,
  rewards per entrance, slowing ratio and braking at the goal are all expressions of whether the
  mouse stops for 1.2 s. They are presented together because the decomposition is the point, not
  because they are independent evidence.
- **Section 2b is independent of 2a**, because its result is an interaction with the number of
  former goals rather than a level at phase 10, and because it is null when the goal is cued.
- **The example mice are typical, not proof.** The three animals in Figure 5 were chosen by a
  stated rule before the figures were drawn — each is closest to its group median across five of
  the measures shown here — and the group panels beside the maps carry the inference.
- **Thresholds are choices.** The braking criteria and the pause definition were set once and not
  adjusted afterwards; their sensitivity has not been tested.
- **Only one per-phase probe result survives correction** for the number of tests run in the full
  report.
- **What the deficit is called is still open.** A capacity limit on holding and testing a set of
  candidate places is consistent with working memory, with pattern separation, or with both, and
  these data cannot separate them.
