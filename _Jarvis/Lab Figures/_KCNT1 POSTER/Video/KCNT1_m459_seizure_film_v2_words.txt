THE BRAIN

             (A 3D reconstruction from the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas)
0:11.1-0:15.3  Inside it, in color: the hippocampus, one on each side.

THE HIPPOCAMPUS
0:16.0-0:20.9  The hippocampus helps us form memories.
             (Its name means “seahorse,” after its curled shape.)
0:21.2-0:26.3  It is also one of the brain's most seizure-prone circuits.

THE MODEL
0:26.5-0:39.0  [The model (a card)]
             Mice with a KCNT1 variant
             1  They carry a change found in people with KCNT1 epilepsy. (Kcnt1 Y777H in mice; KCNT1 Y796H in people)
             2  It makes a potassium channel overactive. (The sodium-activated K+ channel KNa1.1, or Slack)
             3  That quiets inhibitory neurons, the brain’s brakes.
             4  Mice with two copies have frequent seizures, many of them in sleep. (Shore et al., Cell Reports 2020)

HOW WE LISTEN
0:39.5-0:47.8  To listen in, we lower a thin probe with 64 recording sites through the hippocampus.
             (A silicon probe, in an awake mouse)
0:48.6-0:56.3  Each site hears its own layer, from CA1 at the top down through the dentate gyrus.

CELLS AND LAYERS
0:57.0-1:01.5  Granule cells fill the two blades of the dentate gyrus. Their branches reach out to catch signals from the cortex.
             (Real neurons, traced one by one, placed in their layers)
1:01.8-1:07.4  Between the blades, in the hilus, inhibitory cells are the brakes: they reach up to where those signals land and keep granule cells from firing all at once.
             (KCNT1 variants weaken inhibitory cells like these.)
1:07.7-1:12.8  What gets through goes on to CA3, then to CA1, whose cells line up in a layer of their own.
1:13.1-1:17.3  Each kind of cell keeps its body in its own layer, and our probe passes through them all.

AROUND THE LOOP
2:29.5-2:33.7  Past the probe, nothing was recorded. To follow the seizure further, here is the circuit it can travel, as a map.
2:33.9-2:38.8  The entorhinal cortex is the hippocampus’s front door: its upper layers gather signals from across the cortex and send them in.
2:39.0-2:43.9  In the dentate gyrus, granule cells receive them while the hilus’s brake cells keep most of them quiet. The dentate gyrus is a gate.
2:44.1-2:48.2  What gets through goes to CA3, whose cells excite one another, and on to CA1.
2:48.4-2:52.1  CA1 hands it to the subiculum, the hippocampus’s main exit.
2:52.3-2:57.3  The subiculum sends it back to the entorhinal cortex’s deep layers, which pass it out to the cortex and up to the front door again: a loop.
2:57.5-3:03.9  In a seizure, we think the brakes fail. Nothing stops the activity at the gate, so it goes around again and again, each lap stronger.
             (Our hypothesis)
3:04.5-3:10.5  Here is that loop in the mouse’s hippocampus.
             (Drawn, not recorded: the probe saw only CA1 and the dentate gyrus.)
3:10.7-3:17.9  Lap after lap, more of the hippocampus is drawn in.

ACROSS THE BRAIN
3:18.5-3:26.3  The seizure need not stay in the hippocampus. Here is how a seizure like this one can spread across the brain.
             (What is known about how seizures spread; none of it was recorded in this mouse.)
3:26.7-3:35.1  Out through the entorhinal cortex, it reaches the neocortex, the brain’s outer layer.
3:35.5-3:43.3  A bridge of fibers carries it across to the hippocampus on the other side.
3:43.7-3:52.1  Deep in the middle, the thalamus is wired to the whole cortex. Once it joins, both halves of the brain can seize together.
3:52.5-4:01.3  When it reaches the motor cortex, which moves the body, the body jerks in time with it: a convulsive seizure.
             (No video was recorded with this seizure, so we can’t say what mouse 459’s body did.)
4:01.7-4:10.9  If it reaches the brainstem, which holds the body’s posture, the whole body stiffens: the tonic part of a big seizure.

WHAT'S NEXT
4:15.6-4:23.5  Our next question: if we pace these brake cells with light, can we keep a stumble from becoming a seizure?
